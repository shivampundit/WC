🎯 High-Yield Conceptual Pillars & PYQ Exam Traps
  • Central Problem of Scarcity & Choice: Human wants are unlimited and recurring, whereas resources (Land, Labour, Capital, Entrepreneurship) are scarce and have alternative uses. Opportunity Cost is the value of the next best alternative foregone.
  • Production Possibility Curve (PPC / Transformation Curve): Shows maximum attainable combinations of two goods with fixed resources and technology. Points inside PPC = unemployment/inefficiency; points on PPC = full & efficient utilization; points outside PPC = unattainable without resource/tech growth. PPC is concave to the origin when Marginal Opportunity Cost (MOC) is increasing, linear when MOC is constant, and convex when MOC is decreasing.

  • Why the Demand Curve Slopes Downward: (1) Law of Diminishing Marginal Utility (Marshall — consumer equates MU = P), (2) Substitution Effect (Hicks/Slutsky — cheaper good replaces relatively costlier substitutes), (3) Income Effect (fall in price raises real purchasing power P_real), (4) Arrival of new consumers at lower prices, and (5) Multiple uses of a commodity (e.g., electricity/milk).
  • Simultaneous Shifts in Demand & Supply: (1) D↑ and S↑ ⇒ Equilibrium Q* unambiguously rises, while P* is indeterminate (rises if ΔD > ΔS, falls if ΔS > ΔD, constant if ΔD = ΔS); (2) D↑ and S↓ ⇒ Equilibrium P* unambiguously rises, while Q* is indeterminate; (3) D↓ and S↑ ⇒ Equilibrium P* unambiguously falls, while Q* is indeterminate.

1.1 Scarcity, Choice, Opportunity Cost & Production Possibility Curve (PPC)

  • Scarcity vs. Unlimited Wants: Economic problems arise because human wants are unlimited, recurring, and vary in intensity, while productive resources (Land, Labour, Capital, Entrepreneurship) are scarce and have alternative uses.
  • Three Central Problems of an Economy (Paul Samuelson): (1) What to Produce and in What Quantities? (Resource allocation between consumer goods vs. capital goods); (2) How to Produce? (Choice of technique: Labour-intensive vs. Capital-intensive); (3) For Whom to Produce? (Distribution of national income/output among factors of production).
  • Opportunity Cost & Marginal Rate of Transformation (MRT / MOC): Opportunity cost is the value of the next best alternative foregone. Along a Production Possibility Curve (PPC) between Good X and Good Y, MRT_XY = MOC = ΔY Sacrificed ÷ ΔX Gained.
    • Concave PPC (Normal Case): Occurs when MOC/MRT increases as more of Good X is produced because resources are specialized and not equally adaptable to all uses.
    • Straight-Line (Linear) PPC: Occurs when MOC/MRT is constant (resources are perfect substitutes).
    • Convex PPC: Occurs when MOC/MRT decreases (increasing returns/specialization).
    • Points Inside, On & Outside PPC: Any point inside the PPC indicates unemployment or inefficient utilization of resources; any point on the PPC indicates full and efficient utilization; points outside the PPC are unattainable without resource growth or technological progress (which shift the PPC outward).

1.2 Microeconomics vs. Macroeconomics & Determinants of Demand

  • Microeconomics vs. Macroeconomics (Ragnar Frisch, 1933): Microeconomics ('Price Theory', Alfred Marshall) analyzes individual economic units (consumer, firm, industry) under partial equilibrium. Macroeconomics ('Income & Employment Theory', J.M. Keynes) analyzes economy-wide aggregates (National Income, Aggregate Demand/Supply, General Price Level, Employment, Balance of Payments).
  • Effective Demand (3 Pillars): Desire for a commodity + Ability to pay (purchasing power) + Willingness to spend at a specific price and time period.
  • Determinants of Demand (Dx = f(Px, Py, Y, T, E, N)):
    • Own Price (Px): Inverse relation; causes a movement along the demand curve (Extension when P↓, Contraction when P↑).
    • Prices of Related Goods (Py): (a) Substitutes (Tea & Coffee): Positive relation (Price of Tea ↑ ⇒ Demand for Coffee shifts right); (b) Complements (Car & Petrol): Inverse relation (Price of Petrol ↑ ⇒ Demand for Cars shifts left).
    • Consumer Disposable Income (Y): Positive shift for Normal Goods (Ey > 0); negative shift for Inferior Goods (Ey < 0).
    • Tastes, Preferences & Social Effects: Bandwagon/Demonstration effect shifts demand right; Snob effect reduces demand when a good becomes mass-consumed; Veblen effect links prestige directly to high price.

1.3 Downward Slope of Demand, Exceptions & Market Equilibrium Controls

  • Five Reasons Why Demand Slopes Downward: (1) Law of Diminishing Marginal Utility (MU_x = P_x), (2) Hicks–Slutsky Substitution Effect (cheaper good replaces relatively costlier substitutes), (3) Real Income / Purchasing Power Effect, (4) Entry of new marginal buyers at lower prices, and (5) Multiple uses of a commodity.
  • Exceptions to Law of Demand (Upward-Sloping Demand): (1) Giffen Goods (staple inferior goods where negative income effect outweighs substitution effect; all Giffen goods are inferior, but not all inferior goods are Giffen), (2) Veblen / Prestige Goods (conspicuous consumption), (3) Speculative bull runs and war/famine emergencies, and (4) Essential life-saving necessities.
  • Theory of Supply & Price Controls: Supply varies positively with own price (Qs = c + dP) and shifts rightward when input prices fall, technology improves, or subsidies rise (note: Labour supply bends backward at very high wages when the income effect for leisure dominates the substitution effect).
    • Price Ceiling (Maximum Legal Price < P*): Protects consumers (rent control, essential drugs) but creates persistent Excess Demand (Shortage), black marketing, and rationing.
    • Price Floor (Minimum Support Price / Minimum Wage > P*): Protects producers/workers but creates persistent Excess Supply (Surplus) requiring government procurement.

Chapter 02: Elasticity of Demand & Supply

Price, Income & Cross Elasticity, Point/Arc Methods, Total Outlay & Slope

📊 Visual Diagram 2.1: 5 Degrees of Price Elasticity, Point Elasticity on Linear Demand Curve & Total Outlay Matrix
📐 Key Mathematical Formulas, Equations & Equilibrium Conditions
  • Point Price Elasticity of Demand (Ep): (−dQ / dP) × (P / Q) = Lower Segment of Linear Demand Curve ÷ Upper Segment
  • Arc / Midpoint Price Elasticity: [(Q2 − Q1) ÷ ((Q1 + Q2)/2)] ÷ [(P2 − P1) ÷ ((P1 + P2)/2)] = [(ΔQ / ΔP) × ((P1 + P2) / (Q1 + Q2))]
  • Relationship between AR, MR & Elasticity: MR = AR × [1 − (1 / |Ep|)] (At |Ep| = 1, MR = 0 and Total Revenue is Maximum)
  • Cross-Price Elasticity (Ec): (% ΔQx) ÷ (% ΔPy) = (ΔQx / ΔPy) × (Py / Qx) (+ve = Substitutes; −ve = Complements; 0 = Unrelated)
  • Income Elasticity (Ey): (% ΔQ) ÷ (% ΔY) = (ΔQ / ΔY) × (Y / Q) (Ey > 1 Luxury; 0 < Ey < 1 Necessity; Ey < 0 Inferior Good)

🎯 High-Yield Conceptual Pillars & PYQ Exam Traps
  • Slope vs. Elasticity: Slope of demand curve is ΔP/ΔQ, whereas Price Elasticity is (ΔQ/ΔP)×(P/Q) = (1/Slope)×(P/Q). Along a straight-line downward-sloping demand curve, the slope is constant everywhere, but elasticity varies from ∞ at the price axis to 1 at the midpoint to 0 at the quantity axis.
  • Two Intersecting Linear Demand Curves: At the exact point of intersection (same P and Q), the flatter demand curve is more elastic than the steeper demand curve because its inverse slope (dQ/dP) is larger.
  • Determinants of Price Elasticity: Availability of close substitutes (more substitutes ⇒ higher elasticity), proportion of income spent on the good (larger share ⇒ more elastic), nature of commodity (necessities inelastic; luxuries elastic), number of uses (more uses ⇒ more elastic), and time horizon (long-run demand is more elastic than short-run demand).
  • Engel's Law & Income Elasticity: Ernst Engel showed that as household income rises, the percentage of income spent on food declines (0 < Ey < 1 for food necessities), while the share spent on luxuries/services rises (Ey > 1).
  • Elasticity of Supply (Es): Any straight-line supply curve passing through the origin has Es = 1 everywhere regardless of its angle; if it intersects the positive Y-axis (price axis), Es > 1; if it intersects the positive X-axis (quantity axis), Es < 1.

2.1 Price Elasticity of Demand — Measurement Methods & Geometric Rules

  • Four Measurement Methods:
    1. Percentage Method: Ep = (−ΔQ / ΔP) × (P / Q) (for point changes).
    2. Arc / Midpoint Method: Ep = [(Q2 − Q1) ÷ (P2 − P1)] × [(P1 + P2) ÷ (Q1 + Q2)] (eliminates base-choice bias over discrete ranges).
    3. Geometric Point Method on Linear Demand Curve: Ep = Lower Segment ÷ Upper Segment (Y-intercept = ∞; upper half > 1; midpoint = 1; lower half < 1; X-intercept = 0).
    4. Marshall's Total Outlay (TR = P × Q) Method: When P and TR move in opposite directions ⇒ Elastic (Ep > 1); when TR stays constant ⇒ Unitary (Ep = 1); when P and TR move in the same direction ⇒ Inelastic (Ep < 1).
  • Determinants of Price Elasticity: Availability of close substitutes (most important), share of consumer budget spent on the item, necessity vs. luxury nature, number of alternative uses, habit-forming nature, and time horizon (long-run demand is always more elastic than short-run demand).

  • Short Run vs. Long Run: In the short run, at least one factor (capital/plant) is fixed and output varies via the Law of Variable Proportions. In the long run, all factors are variable and scale changes via Returns to Scale (Increasing, Constant, Decreasing).
  • Isoquants (Equal-Product Curves): Downward sloping, convex to origin due to Diminishing Marginal Rate of Technical Substitution (MRTS_LK = −ΔK/ΔL = MPL/MPK), and never intersect.   • Shape of Short-Run Cost Curves: (1) AFC (TFC/Q) is a Rectangular Hyperbola that falls continuously as output expands and never touches either axis; (2) AVC, AC (ATC), and MC are U-shaped due to the Law of Variable Proportions; (3) MC intersects both AVC and AC at their respective minimum points; (4) Minimum point of AVC occurs at a lower output level than the minimum point of AC because falling AFC pulls AC down even after AVC starts rising.
  • Long-Run Average Cost (LAC / Envelope Curve / Planning Curve): Envelopes a family of Short-Run Average Cost (SAC) curves.   • Internal vs. External Economies of Scale: Internal economies (technical, managerial, commercial, financial, risk-bearing) accrue to an individual firm as it expands its own scale (moving along the LAC curve). External economies accrue to all firms in an industry as the entire industry/cluster grows (shifting the entire cost curves downward).
